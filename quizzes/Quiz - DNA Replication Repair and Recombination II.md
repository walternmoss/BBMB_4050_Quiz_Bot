Topic: DNA Replication, Repair, and Recombination II
Quiz Questions

Section 1: Conceptual Practice Questions

Question 1:
Eukaryotic DNA replication involves a coordinated effort of multiple DNA polymerases. Mechanistically explain the distinct roles of DNA Pol alpha, DNA Pol delta, and DNA Pol epsilon in ensuring the complete and accurate replication of both the leading and lagging strands.

Question 2:
Eukaryotic DNA is organized into higher-order chromatin structures, including nucleosomes and 30-nm fibers. During DNA replication, the unwinding of this highly compacted DNA generates significant torsional stress and can lead to entanglement. Describe how Topoisomerase I and Topoisomerase II specifically address these physical challenges to facilitate efficient DNA replication.

Question 3:
The initiation of eukaryotic DNA replication is tightly regulated to ensure that the genome is replicated precisely once per cell cycle. Explain the role of the Origin Recognition Complex (ORC) in this process, specifically detailing how its function contributes to the controlled firing of multiple origins during the S phase.

Question 4:
Consider a scenario where a novel drug is developed that specifically inhibits the reverse transcriptase activity of telomerase in a population of rapidly dividing human cells. Predict and mechanistically explain the long-term consequences of this drug on the cells' ability to maintain their genome integrity and their ultimate proliferative fate.

Question 5:
Telomeres and telomerase are central to two seemingly opposing biological phenomena: cellular senescence and cancer. Discuss the molecular mechanisms by which telomere dynamics and telomerase activity contribute to both the aging process (cellular senescence) and the uncontrolled proliferation characteristic of cancer cells.

Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: Eukaryotic DNA replication utilizes specialized polymerases for initiation and distinct strand synthesis.
Target Answer: DNA Pol alpha initiates eukaryotic DNA replication by synthesizing short RNA/DNA primers on both the leading and lagging strands. This polymerase has primase activity but low processivity. Following primer synthesis, DNA Pol epsilon takes over for the continuous synthesis of the leading strand, exhibiting high processivity due to its association with PCNA. Concurrently, DNA Pol delta is responsible for the discontinuous synthesis of the lagging strand, extending the primers laid down by Pol alpha to form Okazaki fragments, also with high processivity aided by PCNA. This division of labor ensures efficient and accurate replication of the entire eukaryotic genome.

Question 2
Core Principle: Topoisomerases relieve torsional stress and disentangle DNA during replication.
Target Answer: During eukaryotic DNA replication, the unwinding of the DNA double helix and the presence of nucleosomes generate significant positive supercoiling ahead of the replication fork, creating torsional stress. Topoisomerase I addresses this by introducing transient single-strand breaks in the DNA, allowing the DNA strand to rotate around the intact strand, thereby relaxing the supercoils before re-ligation. Topoisomerase II, on the other hand, creates transient double-strand breaks. This activity is crucial for relieving more severe torsional stress, unlinking (decatenating) the intertwined daughter chromosomes after replication, and managing the higher-order chromatin structure, ensuring proper chromosome segregation.

Question 3
Core Principle: The ORC ensures precise, once-per-S-phase initiation of eukaryotic DNA replication.
Target Answer: The Origin Recognition Complex (ORC) is a multi-protein complex that binds to specific DNA sequences at multiple origins of replication throughout the eukaryotic genome. Its binding is a prerequisite for the assembly of other pre-replication complex proteins, effectively "licensing" origins for replication. The activity of ORC and the subsequent firing of origins are tightly regulated by the cell cycle, primarily by cell cycle-dependent kinases. ORC ensures that origins are licensed only during the G1 phase and that once an origin fires during S phase, it cannot be re-licensed or re-fired until the next cell cycle, thereby preventing re-replication of the genome.

Question 4
Core Principle: Telomerase inhibition leads to telomere shortening, impacting cell division and viability.
Target Answer: If a drug inhibits the reverse transcriptase activity of telomerase in rapidly dividing human cells, the telomeres at the ends of the linear chromosomes will progressively shorten with each round of DNA replication. This occurs because conventional DNA polymerases cannot fully replicate the very 3' ends of the lagging strand, leading to a gradual loss of telomeric DNA. As telomeres shorten past a critical length, they lose their protective function, exposing chromosome ends and triggering DNA damage checkpoints. This ultimately leads to cellular senescence, a state of irreversible growth arrest, or programmed cell death, as the cells can no longer maintain genomic integrity or divide indefinitely.

Question 5
Core Principle: Telomere length and telomerase activity are critical determinants of cellular lifespan and cancer progression.
Target Answer: Telomeres and telomerase play a critical, yet contrasting, role in cellular senescence and cancer. In most normal somatic cells, telomerase activity is restricted, leading to progressive shortening of telomeres with each cell division. This telomere shortening acts as a "mitotic clock," eventually signaling the cell to enter cellular senescence, a state of irreversible growth arrest, which is a key mechanism underlying aging. Conversely, cancer cells often reactivate telomerase, allowing them to maintain telomere length indefinitely. This reactivation enables cancer cells to bypass the normal limits on cell division, providing them with replicative immortality and contributing significantly to their uncontrolled proliferation and malignant potential.
