Topic: Viruses and Transposons II
Quiz Questions

Section 1: Conceptual Practice Questions

Question 1:
How do Class I and Class II transposons fundamentally differ in their molecular mechanism of movement within a genome, specifically detailing the intermediates and key enzymatic activities involved in each process?

Question 2:
The integration of retroviral DNA into the host genome by integrase is a critical step in the retroviral life cycle. Mechanistically explain how this process, following reverse transcription, ensures the stable incorporation of the provirus, considering the nature of the DNA intermediate and the role of integrase.

Question 3:
Compare and contrast the regulatory mechanisms that govern the activity of autonomous LINE-1 elements and non-autonomous Alu elements, explaining how Alu elements achieve their mobility despite lacking their own enzymatic machinery.

Question 4:
Azidothymidine (AZT) is an antiretroviral drug targeting reverse transcriptase. Mechanistically explain how AZT inhibits HIV-1 replication, detailing its interaction with reverse transcriptase and the consequence for the viral life cycle.

Question 5:
Retroviruses, such as HIV-1, establish latent proviral reservoirs within host cells. Explain the biological significance of this latent state for both the virus and the host, and discuss the major challenge it poses for effective antiretroviral therapy.

Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: Class I transposons use an RNA intermediate and reverse transcription, while Class II transposons use a direct "cut-and-paste" DNA mechanism.
Target Answer: Class II transposons, or DNA transposons, move via a "cut-and-paste" mechanism without an RNA intermediate. They typically consist of a transposase gene flanked by inverted terminal repeats (ITRs). The transposase enzyme directly excises the DNA element from its original genomic location and inserts it into a new site. In contrast, Class I transposons, or retrotransposons, utilize an RNA intermediate. Their DNA is first transcribed into RNA, which is then reverse transcribed back into DNA by a reverse transcriptase enzyme. This newly synthesized DNA copy is then inserted into a new genomic location, effectively leaving the original copy intact and creating a new one.

Question 2
Core Principle: Integrase stably incorporates the reverse-transcribed dsDNA provirus into the host genome, making it a permanent part of the host's genetic material.
Target Answer: Following reverse transcription of the viral RNA genome into a double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) copy in the cytoplasm, the retroviral integrase enzyme becomes crucial for stable incorporation. Integrase recognizes specific sequences at the ends of the viral dsDNA and catalyzes its insertion into the host cell's chromosomal DNA. This process involves cleaving the host DNA and ligating the viral DNA ends into the gap, forming phosphodiester bonds between the viral and host DNA. Once integrated, this viral DNA, known as a provirus, becomes a permanent part of the host genome and replicates along with the host's own DNA, ensuring its stable inheritance in daughter cells.

Question 3
Core Principle: Autonomous LINE-1 elements encode their own machinery for transposition, while non-autonomous Alu elements co-opt this machinery from LINEs.
Target Answer: Autonomous LINE-1 (L1) elements are capable of transposing independently because they encode the necessary enzymatic machinery: ORF1 (a chaperone) and ORF2 (an endonuclease and reverse transcriptase). These proteins facilitate the transcription of the L1 element into RNA, its reverse transcription into DNA, and subsequent insertion into a new genomic location. In contrast, Alu elements are non-autonomous SINEs, meaning they do not encode their own transposition machinery. Instead, Alu elements rely on and "co-opt" the reverse transcriptase and endonuclease enzymes produced by active LINE-1 elements to facilitate their own movement. Both LINEs and Alu elements utilize an RNA intermediate for their transposition, but only LINEs provide the enzymes for this process.

Question 4
Core Principle: AZT is a nucleoside analog that inhibits reverse transcriptase by acting as a chain terminator during DNA synthesis.
Target Answer: Azidothymidine (AZT) is a nucleoside analog that specifically targets the HIV-1 reverse transcriptase enzyme. AZT is structurally similar to thymidine but contains an azido group (-N3) at the 3' position of its deoxyribose sugar instead of a hydroxyl group (-OH). When AZT is phosphorylated by host cell kinases, it becomes AZT-triphosphate, which can be incorporated into the growing viral DNA strand by reverse transcriptase. However, because the 3'-azido group cannot form a phosphodiester bond with the next incoming nucleotide, the incorporation of AZT acts as a chain terminator. This prematurely halts the synthesis of the viral DNA from its RNA template, thereby preventing the formation of the provirus and disrupting the HIV-1 replication cycle.

Question 5
Core Principle: Latent proviral reservoirs allow retroviruses to persist undetected and replicate with the host genome, posing a significant challenge for eradication.
Target Answer: The establishment of a latent proviral reservoir is biologically significant for HIV-1 because it allows the virus to persist within the host for extended periods without actively replicating or being detected by the immune system. In this latent state, the integrated provirus is transcriptionally silent and replicates passively along with the host cell's DNA, effectively hiding from antiretroviral drugs that primarily target active viral replication. For the host, this means a persistent infection that cannot be cleared by the immune system or current therapies. The major challenge for effective antiretroviral therapy is that these drugs are highly effective against actively replicating virus but cannot eliminate the silent proviral DNA in latent reservoirs. Upon reactivation (often involving Tat and TAR), these latent proviruses can re-initiate lytic replication, leading to a resurgence of viremia and disease progression, necessitating lifelong treatment.
