Topic: Biosynthesis of Membrane Lipids and Sterols I
Quiz Questions

Section 1: Conceptual Practice Questions

Question 1:
Phospholipids are described as amphipathic molecules. Describe the molecular components that contribute to this amphipathic nature and explain the specific role of CDP in the Kennedy pathway for incorporating hydrophilic head groups like choline or ethanolamine into the growing phospholipid.

Question 2:
The Kennedy pathway involves the activation of head groups with CDP. From an energetic perspective, explain why this CDP activation step is crucial for the efficient biosynthesis of phospholipids on the ER membrane.

Question 3:
Cells maintain specific membrane properties and respond to internal signals through phospholipid dynamics. Describe two distinct mechanisms by which cells can modify their phospholipid composition or utilize specific phospholipids for signaling, referencing the Lands' Cycle and phosphatidylserine.

Question 4:
Tay-Sachs disease is a severe neurodegenerative disorder. Mechanistically explain how a deficiency in hexosaminidase A leads to the pathological accumulation of GM2 ganglioside and the subsequent cellular dysfunction observed in nerve cells.

Question 5:

Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: Phospholipids are amphipathic due to distinct hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions, and CDP activates head groups for nucleophilic attack in de novo synthesis.
Target Answer: Phospholipids are amphipathic because they consist of two hydrophobic fatty acid tails and a hydrophilic phosphate head group, all attached to a glycerol backbone. The fatty acid tails are nonpolar and repel water, forming the hydrophobic region. The phosphate head group, often modified with other polar groups like choline or inositol, is charged or highly polar, forming the hydrophilic region. In the Kennedy pathway, CDP (cytidine diphosphate) activates the hydrophilic head group (e.g., CDP-choline). This activation makes the head group a good leaving group, facilitating its transfer to diacylglycerol to form the complete phospholipid, thereby incorporating the hydrophilic portion into the growing molecule.

Question 2
Core Principle: CDP activation provides the necessary free energy for the formation of a new phosphodiester bond in phospholipid synthesis.
Target Answer: The activation of head groups with CDP in the Kennedy pathway is energetically crucial because it creates a high-energy intermediate. The hydrolysis of the pyrophosphate bond in CDP-choline (or CDP-ethanolamine) provides the necessary free energy to drive the formation of the new phosphodiester bond between the activated head group and diacylglycerol. This makes the reaction thermodynamically favorable, allowing for the efficient and irreversible synthesis of phospholipids on the ER membrane.

Question 3
Core Principle: Cells remodel phospholipids via Lands' Cycle and use PS flipping for apoptosis signaling.
Target Answer: Cells dynamically adjust their phospholipid composition through the Lands' Cycle (remodeling). This process involves the removal of a specific fatty acid from a phospholipid by a phospholipase, creating a lysophospholipid, followed by its replacement with a different fatty acid by an acyltransferase. This allows for fine-tuning of membrane properties like fluidity or curvature. Additionally, phosphatidylserine (PS) serves as a critical signaling molecule. Under normal conditions, PS is primarily located in the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane. However, during apoptosis, PS flips to the outer membrane leaflet, acting as an "eat me" signal for phagocytic cells, thus initiating the clearance of apoptotic cells.

Question 4
Core Principle: Hexosaminidase A deficiency leads to GM2 ganglioside accumulation, causing neurodegeneration.
Target Answer: Tay-Sachs disease is caused by a deficiency in the lysosomal enzyme hexosaminidase A. This enzyme is responsible for the degradation of GM2 ganglioside. Without functional hexosaminidase A, GM2 ganglioside cannot be properly broken down and accumulates to toxic levels within the lysosomes of nerve cells. This accumulation disrupts normal cellular function, leading to progressive neurodegeneration, which manifests as the severe neurological symptoms characteristic of Tay-Sachs disease.

Question 5
Core Principle: PC, PI, and PS have distinct structures and localizations that enable their specialized roles in membranes, signaling, and apoptosis.
Target Answer: Phosphatidylcholine (PC) is the most abundant phospholipid and serves as a major component of lung surfactant, highlighting its role in reducing surface tension in the alveoli. Phosphatidylinositol (PI) is critical for cell signaling; it can be phosphorylated to generate various phosphoinositides that act as second messengers, recruiting proteins to the membrane and initiating signaling cascades. Phosphatidylserine (PS) is typically found on the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane, but its regulated flipping to the outer leaflet serves as an "eat me" signal for apoptosis, facilitating the removal of dying cells. These examples demonstrate how specific phospholipid structures and their dynamic localization contribute to diverse and essential cellular functions beyond just forming the lipid bilayer.
