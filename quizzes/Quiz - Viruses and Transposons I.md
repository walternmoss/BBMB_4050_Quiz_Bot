Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: Enveloped virions utilize a protein capsid for genome protection and a host-derived lipid bilayer with viral glycoproteins for host cell attachment and entry.
Target Answer: An enveloped virion protects its genetic material primarily through its capsid, a protein shell that encases the viral genome, shielding it from environmental degradation and physical damage. For transmission and infection of new host cells, the virion utilizes a host-derived lipid bilayer membrane, known as the envelope, which surrounds the capsid. Embedded within this envelope are viral glycoproteins. These glycoproteins are crucial for specific attachment to receptors on the surface of target host cells, initiating the infection process. The combination of the protective capsid and the envelope with its specific glycoproteins ensures both the integrity of the viral genome during extracellular transit and its efficient delivery into a new susceptible host cell.

Question 2
Core Principle: Viral protein self-assembly into complex geometric shapes is a thermodynamically favorable process that ensures efficient and stable virion formation.
Target Answer: The self-assembly of viral proteins into complex geometric shapes is biologically significant because it represents an efficient and robust mechanism for virion formation. This process is often spontaneous, driven by the intrinsic properties of the viral proteins, which allows for the rapid and accurate construction of the capsid without requiring extensive cellular energy or complex host machinery. The resulting stable, geometrically precise structure provides robust protection for the viral genome against physical and chemical stresses in the extracellular environment, ensuring its integrity during transmission between host cells. This inherent efficiency in assembly is vital for the rapid production of new infectious particles during the viral replication cycle.

Question 3
Core Principle: Viruses employ diverse strategies, such as host machinery redirection or latent episomal persistence, to manage their genetic material and gene expression.
Target Answer: Bacteriophage T4 and Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV) employ distinct strategies for managing their genetic material and gene expression within a host cell. Bacteriophage T4, upon infecting bacteria, redirects the host RNA polymerase using viral proteins like AsiA and MotA. This mechanism ensures that the host's transcriptional machinery is primarily used to transcribe T4's own genes, thereby hijacking the cell's resources for its lytic replication cycle. In contrast, EBV, a herpesvirus, establishes a lifelong latent infection in B cells. During latency, its genome persists as a chromatinized episome, which is an autonomously replicating circular DNA molecule. This episome replicates along with the host chromosomes, ensuring its stable inheritance by daughter cells without necessarily producing new infectious virions, thus allowing for long-term persistence within the host.

Question 4
Core Principle: Subgenomic RNAs are essential for expressing specific viral proteins, and their disruption would impair the production of those proteins and subsequent virion assembly.
Target Answer: SARS-CoV-2, as a (+)ssRNA virus, relies on subgenomic RNAs (sgRNAs) to express its various structural and accessory proteins, which are distinct from the initial polyprotein translated directly from the genomic RNA. If a mutation prevented the efficient production of sgRNAs, the virus would be unable to synthesize the specific proteins encoded by these sgRNAs, such as the spike glycoprotein or nucleoprotein. These structural proteins are critical components required for the assembly of new virions, including the formation of the capsid and the acquisition of the envelope. Consequently, the inability to produce these essential proteins would severely impair the assembly of new infectious particles, significantly hindering the virus's ability to replicate and spread to new cells.

Question 5
Core Principle: Antigenic shift in Influenza A involves major genetic reassortment, leading to novel viral strains that evade pre-existing host immunity.
Target Answer: Antigenic shift in Influenza A virus occurs when two distinct strains of the segmented (-)ssRNA virus co-infect the same host cell. During the replication process, the segmented genomes from both viral strains can undergo reassortment, meaning that new virions can be packaged with a novel combination of gene segments from both parental strains. This leads to a drastic change in the viral surface proteins, particularly the hemagglutinin and neuraminidase, which are the primary targets of the host immune system. Clinically and evolutionarily, this phenomenon is highly significant because the resulting novel strain can evade pre-existing immune memory in the human population. Antibodies developed against previous strains are no longer effective against the new variant, leading to a lack of population immunity, widespread infection, severe disease, and potentially global pandemics.
