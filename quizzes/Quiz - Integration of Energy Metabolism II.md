Topic: Integration of Energy Metabolism II
Quiz Questions

Section 1: Conceptual Practice Questions

Question 1:
The fed state is characterized by high insulin levels. Mechanistically explain how this hormonal signal promotes energy storage by describing the key metabolic pathways that are *promoted* and *inhibited* in the liver and muscle during this state, and the primary molecules involved in storage.

Question 2:
During prolonged starvation, the body shifts its primary fuel source for the brain. Describe the metabolic shift that occurs in the liver to provide this alternative fuel, explaining why this adaptation is critical for sparing glucose.

Question 3:
Compare and contrast the primary hormonal signals (insulin and glucagon) and their resulting metabolic effects on glucose homeostasis in the liver during the fed state versus the fasting state.

Question 4:
In Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, cells exhibit insulin resistance. Explain the direct metabolic consequences of insulin resistance on glucose uptake and energy storage pathways in peripheral tissues (like muscle and adipose tissue) and the liver, leading to the observed hyperglycemia.

Question 5:
During sustained exercise, the body transitions its primary fuel sources. Describe the sequence of fuel utilization, starting from the initial phase and progressing to prolonged activity, and identify the key organs or tissues primarily responsible for providing these fuels.

Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: High insulin in the fed state promotes glucose uptake and storage while inhibiting glucose production and fatty acid breakdown.
Target Answer: In the fed state, high insulin levels signal abundant energy. This promotes glucose uptake into insulin-sensitive tissues like muscle and adipose tissue. In the liver and muscle, insulin stimulates glycogen synthesis, converting excess glucose into glycogen for storage. Concurrently, insulin promotes triacylglycerol (TAG) synthesis, storing excess energy as fat, particularly in adipose tissue. To prevent unnecessary glucose production and fatty acid breakdown, insulin inhibits gluconeogenesis and fatty acid oxidation in the liver.

Question 2
Core Principle: During starvation, the liver produces ketone bodies from fatty acids to fuel the brain, thereby sparing glucose for other essential tissues.
Target Answer: During prolonged starvation, glycogen stores are depleted, and gluconeogenesis becomes the primary source of glucose. To spare this limited glucose for cells that absolutely require it (e.g., red blood cells), the liver significantly increases fatty acid oxidation. This process leads to the production of ketone bodies, which are then released into the bloodstream. The brain, which typically relies on glucose, adapts to utilize these ketone bodies as a major alternative fuel source, thus reducing its demand for glucose and preserving the body's remaining glucose reserves.

Question 3
Core Principle: Insulin dominates in the fed state, promoting glucose storage, while glucagon dominates in the fasting state, promoting glucose mobilization.
Target Answer: In the fed state, high insulin levels are the primary hormonal signal. Insulin promotes glucose uptake and stimulates glycogen synthesis in the liver, storing excess glucose. It also inhibits gluconeogenesis, preventing the liver from producing new glucose. Conversely, in the fasting state, insulin levels are low, and glucagon levels are high. Glucagon acts on the liver to stimulate glycogenolysis, breaking down stored glycogen to release glucose. It also strongly stimulates gluconeogenesis, synthesizing new glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors to maintain blood glucose homeostasis.

Question 4
Core Principle: Insulin resistance prevents cells from responding to insulin, leading to impaired glucose uptake and storage, and unchecked glucose production, resulting in hyperglycemia.
Target Answer: Insulin resistance, characteristic of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, means that cells in peripheral tissues (like muscle and adipose) and the liver do not respond effectively to insulin signals. Consequently, glucose uptake into muscle and adipose tissue is impaired, leading to less glucose being removed from the bloodstream. In the liver, insulin's inhibitory effect on gluconeogenesis is diminished, allowing the liver to continue producing glucose even when blood glucose levels are high. This combination of reduced glucose uptake by peripheral tissues and sustained glucose production by the liver results in persistent hyperglycemia.

Question 5
Core Principle: Exercise initially uses muscle glycogen, then blood glucose, and eventually shifts to fatty acids as the primary fuel source.
Target Answer: During the initial phase of exercise, muscle glycogen serves as the primary and readily available fuel source for muscle contraction. As exercise continues and muscle glycogen stores begin to deplete, glucose uptake by the muscles increases, utilizing circulating blood glucose. For prolonged or sustained exercise, the body transitions to fatty acids as the major energy source. These fatty acids are primarily mobilized from adipose tissue stores and undergo fatty acid oxidation to generate ATP, sparing remaining glucose for other essential functions.
