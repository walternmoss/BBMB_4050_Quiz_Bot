Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: PSII uses light energy absorbed by P680 to split water, releasing electrons, protons, and oxygen.
Target Answer: In Photosystem II (PSII), the special pair P680 absorbs light energy, becoming excited. This excitation enables P680 to extract electrons from water molecules. The process, known as photolysis or water splitting, yields four protons (4H+), four electrons (4e-), and one molecule of oxygen (O2) from two molecules of water (2H2O). These extracted electrons are then passed along the electron transport chain.

Question 2
Core Principle: The Z-scheme illustrates electron flow down a redox potential gradient, and this flow drives proton accumulation in the lumen.
Target Answer: The Z-scheme is a conceptual diagram that illustrates the pathway of electron flow from water to NADP+ based on the redox potential of the electron carriers. Electrons are initially at a high redox potential (low energy) in water, are boosted by PSII, then flow downhill energetically through the cytochrome b6f complex, are boosted again by PSI, and finally flow to NADP+ at a lower redox potential (higher energy). This electron flow, particularly through components like the cytochrome b6f complex, is coupled to the active transport of protons from the stroma into the thylakoid lumen, leading to a significant accumulation of protons within the lumen.

Question 3
Core Principle: Both PSII and PSI are required in sequence to provide the necessary energy boosts to move electrons from water to the high-energy state of NADPH.
Target Answer: The sequential action of both Photosystem II (PSII) and Photosystem I (PSI) is essential because each photosystem provides a distinct energetic boost to the electrons. PSII is responsible for extracting electrons from water and providing the initial energy input. However, the energy level of these electrons after PSII is not sufficient to reduce NADP+. PSI then provides a second input of light energy, re-energizing the electrons to a higher potential, which is necessary for their transfer to ferredoxin and subsequent reduction of NADP+ to NADPH by Ferredoxin-NADP+ Reductase. Neither photosystem alone can span the entire redox potential difference required to move electrons from water to NADP+.

Question 4
Core Principle: Inhibiting plastoquinone blocks electron flow from PSII to PSI, preventing both NADPH production and proton accumulation.
Target Answer: If plastoquinone were inhibited from transferring electrons to the cytochrome b6f complex, the electron flow from Photosystem II (PSII) would be completely blocked. Consequently, electrons would not reach Photosystem I (PSI) or Ferredoxin-NADP+ Reductase, leading to a complete cessation of NADPH production. Furthermore, the electron transport through the cytochrome b6f complex, which is responsible for pumping protons into the thylakoid lumen, would cease. This would prevent the accumulation of protons in the lumen, thereby abolishing the proton gradient essential for ATP synthesis (though ATP synthesis is not explicitly mentioned in the provided text, proton accumulation is).

Question 5
Core Principle: The thylakoid membrane and lumen provide distinct compartments for the light reactions, and chloroplasts originated from endosymbiosis.
Target Answer: The chloroplast's distinct structural organization, particularly the thylakoid membrane and lumen, is critical for the light reactions. The thylakoid membranes provide the extensive surface area necessary to embed the photosystems (PSII and PSI), electron transport chain components (plastoquinone, cytochrome b6f complex, plastocyanin), and Ferredoxin-NADP+ Reductase. The thylakoid lumen, the internal aqueous space, is where protons accumulate as a result of water splitting and electron transport. This spatial separation allows for the establishment of a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane, which is fundamental for energy capture. The chloroplast itself is believed to have originated from an endosymbiotic event involving a cyanobacterium, explaining its double membrane and internal thylakoid structure, which resembles its prokaryotic ancestor.
