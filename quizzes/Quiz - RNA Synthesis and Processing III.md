Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: Spliceosomal splicing involves specific snRNA recognition of pre-mRNA sites to excise introns and ligate exons.
Target Answer: The spliceosome, composed of snRNAs (U1, U2, U4, U5, U6) and proteins, initiates splicing by recognizing specific sequences on the pre-mRNA. U1 snRNA recognizes the 5' splice site, while U2 snRNA recognizes the branch point. Subsequent rearrangements involving U4, U5, and U6 snRNAs bring the splice sites together, leading to the formation of a lariat intermediate from the intron. This lariat is then excised, and the flanking exons are ligated together, resulting in mature mRNA.

Question 2
Core Principle: Different splicing mechanisms utilize distinct chemical initiators or cofactors to catalyze phosphodiester bond rearrangements.
Target Answer: Group I introns initiate splicing using an external guanosine cofactor, which attacks the 5' splice site. In contrast, Group II introns and spliceosomal splicing both proceed via a similar chemical mechanism involving an internal adenosine residue within the intron's branch point. This adenosine's 2'-hydroxyl group attacks the 5' splice site, forming a lariat intermediate. While Group II introns are self-splicing, spliceosomal splicing requires the complex machinery of the spliceosome (snRNAs and proteins) to facilitate these precise bond rearrangements and lariat formation.

Question 3
Core Principle: Alternative splicing of SMN2 leads to a truncated protein, contributing to the SMA disease phenotype.
Target Answer: In Spinal Muscular Atrophy (SMA), the SMN1 gene typically produces full-length, functional SMN protein. However, the paralogous SMN2 gene undergoes alternative splicing, which predominantly leads to the exclusion of an exon. This alternative splicing event results in the production of mostly truncated SMN protein, which is non-functional or less stable. The insufficient amount of functional SMN protein due to this altered splicing of SMN2 is the underlying cause of the disease phenotype in SMA.

Question 4
Core Principle: Spinraza is an antisense oligonucleotide that corrects SMN2 alternative splicing to increase functional SMN protein.
Target Answer: Spinraza is an antisense oligonucleotide designed to treat SMA by modulating the splicing of the SMN2 gene. Its mechanism involves binding to the SMN2 pre-mRNA, which alters the splicing pattern. Specifically, Spinraza promotes the inclusion of the exon that is typically excluded during SMN2 alternative splicing. This correction in splicing leads to an increased production of full-length, functional SMN protein from the SMN2 gene, thereby mitigating the effects of the SMN1 mutation and improving the disease outcome for SMA patients.

Question 5
Core Principle: RNA editing of ApoB-100 mRNA in the intestine creates a stop codon, producing a shorter, functionally distinct protein.
Target Answer: In the context of ApoB-100 mRNA, RNA editing occurs specifically in the intestine. This post-transcriptional modification involves a cytidine deaminase enzyme, which converts a specific cytidine residue within the ApoB-100 mRNA sequence into a uridine. This C-to-U change creates a premature stop codon in the mRNA. Consequently, translation terminates prematurely, resulting in the production of a shorter protein called ApoB-48, which has a distinct physiological role in lipid metabolism compared to the full-length ApoB-100 produced in the liver from the unedited transcript.
