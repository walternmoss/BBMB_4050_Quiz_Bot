Topic: Fatty Acid Metabolism I
Quiz Questions

Section 1: Conceptual Practice Questions

Question 1:
Describe the four sequential enzymatic steps of beta-oxidation for a saturated fatty acyl-CoA, and identify the specific products generated in each complete cycle.

Question 2:
The beta-oxidation pathway generates reduced cofactors, FADH2 and NADH. Explain the fundamental role of these molecules in the overall process of cellular energy generation, as implied by their production in the mitochondria.

Question 3:
Detail the hormonal signaling pathway that initiates the mobilization of stored triacylglycerols from adipose tissue, specifically naming the hormones, key enzymes, and regulatory proteins involved in the breakdown process.

Question 4:
A genetic mutation results in a non-functional Carnitine Palmitoyltransferase I (CPT I). Mechanistically explain why this defect would specifically impair the ability of cells to oxidize long-chain fatty acids for energy, particularly during prolonged fasting.

Question 5:
Explain how fatty acids, once mobilized from adipose tissue, are transported through the bloodstream and subsequently enter the mitochondrial matrix for oxidation, highlighting the roles of specific transport molecules and complexes.

Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: Beta-oxidation is a cyclic process that systematically shortens fatty acids by two carbons, producing acetyl-CoA and reduced cofactors.
Target Answer: The beta-oxidation pathway involves four sequential enzymatic steps: first, an oxidation step catalyzed by FAD-dependent acyl-CoA dehydrogenase, producing FADH2; second, a hydration step; third, a second oxidation step catalyzed by NAD+-dependent hydroxylacyl-CoA dehydrogenase, producing NADH; and fourth, a thiolysis step, which cleaves off an Acetyl-CoA unit. In each complete cycle, a saturated fatty acyl-CoA undergoes these transformations, resulting in the release of one Acetyl-CoA molecule, one FADH2, and one NADH. This cyclic process continues, progressively shortening the fatty acid chain by two carbons per cycle, until the entire molecule is converted into Acetyl-CoA units.

Question 2
Core Principle: FADH2 and NADH are electron carriers generated during beta-oxidation that transfer electrons to the electron transport chain for ATP synthesis.
Target Answer: FADH2 and NADH are crucial reduced cofactors produced during the oxidative steps of beta-oxidation within the mitochondrial matrix. FADH2 is generated during the initial oxidation step, and NADH is generated during the third oxidation step of each cycle. These molecules serve as carriers of high-energy electrons. Their significance lies in their ability to donate these electrons to the electron transport chain, which is located on the inner mitochondrial membrane. The flow of electrons through this chain drives the pumping of protons, establishing an electrochemical gradient that is ultimately used to synthesize ATP, the primary energy currency of the cell.

Question 3
Core Principle: Hormones like glucagon and epinephrine trigger lipolysis in adipose tissue, releasing fatty acids for energy.
Target Answer: The mobilization of stored triacylglycerols from adipose tissue is initiated by hormonal signals such as glucagon or epinephrine, which signal a need for energy. These hormones bind to receptors on adipocytes, triggering a signaling cascade that leads to the phosphorylation and activation of Perilipin. Activated Perilipin then allows Hormone-Sensitive Lipase (HSL) to access and hydrolyze triacylglycerols. Additionally, Adipose Triacylglycerol Lipase (ATGL) initiates the breakdown of triacylglycerols, and Monoglyceride Lipase (MGL) completes the hydrolysis of monoglycerides. This coordinated action of lipases breaks down stored triacylglycerols into fatty acids and monoglycerides, which are then released from the adipose tissue into the bloodstream.

Question 4
Core Principle: A non-functional CPT I prevents fatty acyl-CoA from entering the mitochondrial matrix for beta-oxidation.
Target Answer: Carnitine Palmitoyltransferase I (CPT I) is a critical enzyme located on the outer mitochondrial membrane, responsible for the conversion of long-chain acyl-CoA into acylcarnitine. This conversion is an essential step in the carnitine shuttle, which is the primary mechanism for transporting long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix, where beta-oxidation occurs. If CPT I is non-functional due to a genetic mutation, acyl-CoA cannot be efficiently converted to acylcarnitine, and thus cannot be transported across the inner mitochondrial membrane. Consequently, long-chain fatty acids are unable to enter the mitochondrial matrix to undergo beta-oxidation. During prolonged fasting, when the body heavily relies on fatty acid oxidation as a major source of energy, this defect would severely impair ATP production, leading to a profound energy deficit and metabolic dysfunction.

Question 5
Core Principle: Albumin transports fatty acids in the blood, and the carnitine shuttle facilitates their entry into mitochondria for oxidation.
Target Answer: Once fatty acids are mobilized from adipose tissue through lipolysis, they are released into the bloodstream. Due to their hydrophobic nature, these fatty acids cannot travel freely in the aqueous blood plasma. Instead, they bind to serum albumin, a protein that acts as a carrier, transporting them to various peripheral tissues that require energy. Upon reaching target cells, fatty acids are activated to acyl-CoA in the cytoplasm. For these acyl-CoAs to be oxidized, they must enter the mitochondrial matrix. This entry is facilitated by the carnitine shuttle system, where Carnitine Palmitoyltransferase I (CPT I) converts acyl-CoA to acylcarnitine, allowing it to traverse the inner mitochondrial membrane. This coordinated transport by albumin and the carnitine shuttle ensures efficient delivery and utilization of fatty acids for energy production via beta-oxidation.
