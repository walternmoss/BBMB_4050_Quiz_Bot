Topic: Control of Gene Expression II
Quiz Questions

Section 1: Conceptual Practice Questions

Question 1:
How do histone acetylation and DNA methylation mechanistically alter chromatin structure to influence gene accessibility for the transcriptional machinery?

Question 2:
Consider the physical state of chromatin. How do epigenetic modifications like histone acetylation and DNA methylation influence the *stability* and *compactness* of chromatin, and what are the direct physical consequences for the binding of General Transcription Factors (GTFs)?

Question 3:
Eukaryotic gene expression requires precise integration of multiple signals. Explain how Specific Transcription Factors (STFs), the Mediator complex, and General Transcription Factors (GTFs) collaboratively establish a regulated transcriptional state, highlighting the role of each component in this regulatory cascade.

Question 4:
A researcher identifies a novel compound that specifically inhibits the enzyme responsible for histone acetylation. Predict and mechanistically explain the likely impact of this compound on the expression of genes typically found in euchromatin.

Question 5:
Aberrant epigenetic modifications are implicated in disease. Mechanistically explain how the silencing of tumor suppressor genes via DNA methylation contributes to the development of cancer, linking this epigenetic change to its functional consequence at the cellular level.

Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: Histone acetylation relaxes chromatin, while DNA methylation compacts it, directly altering DNA accessibility.
Target Answer: Histone acetylation involves the addition of acetyl groups to lysine residues on histone proteins, which neutralizes their positive charge and reduces their affinity for the negatively charged DNA. This leads to a more relaxed, open chromatin structure (euchromatin), making the DNA more accessible for the transcriptional machinery. Conversely, DNA methylation typically occurs on cytosine residues within CpG dinucleotides and is associated with gene silencing. This modification can recruit proteins that promote chromatin condensation, leading to a more compact, inaccessible heterochromatin state, thereby preventing transcription factor binding and RNA Polymerase II recruitment.

Question 2
Core Principle: Epigenetic modifications alter chromatin's physical stability and compactness, directly affecting GTF access.
Target Answer: Histone acetylation reduces the positive charge of histones, weakening their electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged DNA. This leads to a less stable, more relaxed, and less compact chromatin structure (euchromatin). In this physically open state, General Transcription Factors (GTFs) can more readily bind to promoter regions. Conversely, DNA methylation, particularly in CpG dinucleotides, is associated with a more compact and stable heterochromatin state. This physical compaction makes the DNA less accessible, sterically hindering the binding of GTFs and RNA Polymerase II to the promoter, thereby repressing gene expression.

Question 3
Core Principle: STFs bind enhancers/silencers, recruit the Mediator complex, which then bridges to GTFs and RNA Pol II to regulate transcription.
Target Answer: Specific Transcription Factors (STFs) initiate the regulatory process by binding to specific DNA sequences called enhancers or silencers, which can be located far from the promoter. Upon binding, STFs recruit the Mediator complex. The Mediator complex acts as a crucial physical bridge, facilitating communication and interaction between the distally bound STFs and the General Transcription Factors (GTFs) that are assembled at the core promoter. The GTFs, in turn, are responsible for recruiting RNA Polymerase II to the promoter, forming the pre-initiation complex. This collaborative interaction ensures that transcription is initiated or repressed in a highly regulated manner, integrating signals from various STFs.

Question 4
Core Principle: Inhibiting histone acetylation will lead to chromatin compaction and reduced gene expression.
Target Answer: Histone acetylation typically relaxes chromatin, making genes in euchromatin accessible for transcription. If a compound inhibits the enzyme responsible for histone acetylation, histones will remain in a deacetylated state. This will maintain or increase the positive charge on histones, strengthening their electrostatic interactions with DNA. Consequently, the chromatin structure will become more compact and condensed, resembling heterochromatin. This reduced accessibility will sterically hinder the binding of General Transcription Factors (GTFs) and RNA Polymerase II to the promoters of genes typically found in euchromatin, leading to a significant decrease in their transcriptional expression.

Question 5
Core Principle: DNA methylation can silence tumor suppressor genes, removing a critical brake on cell proliferation and promoting cancer.
Target Answer: Tumor suppressor genes normally function to regulate cell growth, division, and apoptosis, acting as critical brakes on uncontrolled cellular proliferation. Aberrant DNA methylation, specifically the methylation of cytosine in CpG dinucleotides within the promoter regions of these tumor suppressor genes, leads to their silencing. This epigenetic modification creates a compact, inaccessible chromatin state, preventing the binding of transcription factors and RNA Polymerase II, thereby inhibiting the production of the tumor suppressor proteins. The functional consequence is the loss of critical cellular control mechanisms, allowing cells to divide unchecked, accumulate further mutations, and evade programmed cell death, which are hallmarks of cancer development.
