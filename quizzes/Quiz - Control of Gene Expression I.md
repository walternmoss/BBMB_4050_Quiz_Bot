Topic: Control of Gene Expression I
Quiz Questions

Section 1: Conceptual Practice Questions

Question 1:
How do sigma factors ensure the specificity of gene transcription in prokaryotes, and what is the consequence of having different sigma factors available to the cell?

Question 2:
Explain the physical basis by which mRNA stem-loop structures, in conjunction with ribosome movement, lead to premature transcription termination during attenuation.

Question 3:
Compare and contrast the regulatory logic of an inducible operon (like the *lac* operon) with a repressible operon (like the *trp* operon), specifically detailing how an environmental signal switches each operon's expression state.

Question 4:
A bacterial cell experiences a sudden and severe amino acid starvation. Mechanistically explain how the stringent response, involving alarmones, alters the cell's transcriptional priorities to cope with this stress.

Question 5:
Describe how quorum sensing allows a bacterial population to coordinate a global response, such as biofilm formation, and explain the clinical significance of this coordinated behavior.

Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: Sigma factors confer promoter specificity to RNA polymerase, enabling the transcription of distinct gene sets.
Target Answer: Sigma factors are specialized proteins that bind to RNA polymerase, forming a holoenzyme. This holoenzyme then specifically recognizes and binds to particular promoter sequences upstream of genes. Different sigma factors exist, each recognizing a unique set of promoter sequences. This allows the bacterium to rapidly switch its transcriptional program, for example, by utilizing a specific sigma factor to activate genes required for a heat shock response, thereby transcribing only the genes relevant to the current environmental condition.

Question 2
Core Principle: Attenuation relies on the formation of alternative mRNA stem-loop structures, influenced by ribosome speed, to prematurely terminate transcription.
Target Answer: Attenuation is a mechanism of premature transcription termination that depends on the coupling of transcription and translation in prokaryotes. The leader sequence of an attenuating operon mRNA can fold into alternative stem-loop structures. One specific stem-loop structure, often called the terminator loop, signals for RNA polymerase to dissociate from the DNA template, thereby terminating transcription. The formation of this terminator loop is influenced by the speed of the ribosome translating a short leader peptide. If the ribosome stalls due to low concentrations of specific amino acids, it prevents the formation of an anti-terminator loop, allowing the terminator loop to form and prematurely stop transcription.

Question 3
Core Principle: Inducible operons are activated by an inducer, while repressible operons are deactivated by a corepressor, both modulating repressor protein activity.
Target Answer: Inducible operons, like the *lac* operon, are typically "off" because a repressor protein is constitutively bound to the operator, physically blocking RNA polymerase from transcribing the structural genes. When an inducer molecule, such as allolactose, is present, it binds to the repressor protein, causing a conformational change that reduces the repressor's affinity for the operator. This allows RNA polymerase to bind to the promoter and initiate transcription, turning the operon "on." In contrast, repressible operons, like the *trp* operon, are typically "on" because the repressor protein is inactive and does not bind to the operator. When a corepressor molecule, such as tryptophan, is abundant, it binds to the repressor protein, activating it. The activated repressor then binds to the operator, blocking RNA polymerase and turning the operon "off," thus preventing the unnecessary synthesis of tryptophan when it is already plentiful.

Question 4
Core Principle: The stringent response uses alarmones (ppGpp) to globally reduce rRNA transcription during amino acid starvation.
Target Answer: During severe amino acid starvation, the stringent response is triggered. This global regulatory mechanism involves the synthesis of signaling molecules called alarmones, specifically ppGpp. When amino acids are scarce, uncharged tRNAs accumulate and bind to the ribosomal A-site, which signals for the synthesis of ppGpp. The alarmone ppGpp then acts to reduce the transcription of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes. By decreasing rRNA synthesis, the cell reduces the production of new ribosomes, thereby conserving energy and resources that would otherwise be used for protein synthesis, allowing the cell to prioritize survival during nutrient deprivation.

Question 5
Core Principle: Quorum sensing enables bacteria to coordinate population-wide behaviors, like biofilm formation, through autoinducer molecules, which has significant clinical implications.
Target Answer: Quorum sensing is a cell-density-dependent regulatory mechanism where bacteria communicate and coordinate gene expression based on their population size. Individual bacterial cells produce and release small signaling molecules called autoinducers into the environment. As the bacterial population density increases, the concentration of these autoinducers in the extracellular environment also rises. Once the autoinducer concentration reaches a threshold level, it triggers a global change in gene expression within the bacterial community. A significant clinical consequence of quorum sensing is its role in biofilm formation. Biofilms are structured bacterial communities that adhere to surfaces, including medical devices. The coordinated gene expression facilitated by quorum sensing allows bacteria within a biofilm to exhibit increased resistance to host immune defenses and antibiotics, making biofilm-associated infections particularly difficult to treat.
