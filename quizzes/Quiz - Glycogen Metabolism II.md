Topic: Glycogen Metabolism II
Quiz Questions

Section 1: Conceptual Practice Questions

Question 1:
How do the opposing actions of protein kinase A (PKA) and protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) precisely control the activity states of both glycogen synthase and glycogen phosphorylase in response to hormonal signals?

Question 2:
The study guide emphasizes "reciprocal regulation" to prevent a "futile cycle." Mechanistically explain how the allosteric regulation by AMP, ATP, and glucose-6-phosphate in muscle tissue, combined with hormonal signaling, ensures that energy is not wasted by simultaneously synthesizing and breaking down glycogen.

Question 3:
Describe the complete signaling cascade initiated by glucagon binding to its liver receptor, detailing how this ultimately leads to increased glycogen breakdown and inhibited glycogen synthesis. Identify the key molecular intermediates and their roles.

Question 4:
Imagine a genetic mutation in liver cells that causes protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) to be constitutively active, even in the absence of insulin signaling. Predict and mechanistically explain the likely impact on liver glycogen levels and systemic blood glucose homeostasis during a fasting state.

Question 5:
Explain how the distinct allosteric and hormonal regulatory mechanisms in liver versus muscle tissue reflect their different physiological roles in maintaining overall glucose homeostasis and responding to acute energy demands.

Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: PKA and PP1 exert reciprocal control over glycogen synthase and phosphorylase through phosphorylation and dephosphorylation, respectively.
Target Answer: PKA phosphorylates both glycogen phosphorylase (activating it) and glycogen synthase (inhibiting it). Conversely, PP1 dephosphorylates both glycogen phosphorylase (inhibiting it) and glycogen synthase (activating it). This ensures that a single hormonal signal (e.g., insulin activating PP1 or glucagon/epinephrine activating PKA) can simultaneously promote one pathway while inhibiting the other, preventing futile cycling and coordinating glycogen metabolism.

Question 2
Core Principle: Allosteric effectors like AMP, ATP, and glucose-6-phosphate, alongside hormonal signals, ensure that glycogen synthesis and breakdown are reciprocally regulated to prevent energy waste.
Target Answer: In muscle, AMP signals low energy and allosterically activates glycogen phosphorylase, promoting breakdown to generate glucose-6-phosphate for energy. Conversely, high ATP and glucose-6-phosphate signal high energy and allosterically inhibit glycogen phosphorylase, preventing unnecessary breakdown. This allosteric control, combined with hormonal signals like epinephrine activating PKA to phosphorylate and activate phosphorylase, ensures that glycogen is only broken down when energy is needed, preventing a futile cycle where energy would be wasted by simultaneously synthesizing and breaking down glycogen.

Question 3
Core Principle: Glucagon initiates a G protein-mediated cAMP/PKA cascade in the liver, leading to phosphorylation-dependent activation of glycogen breakdown and inhibition of synthesis.
Target Answer: When glucagon binds to its receptor on liver cells, it activates a G protein. This activated G protein then increases intracellular cAMP levels. cAMP, in turn, activates protein kinase A (PKA). PKA then phosphorylates two key enzymes: it phosphorylates and activates glycogen phosphorylase, thereby stimulating glycogen breakdown. Simultaneously, PKA phosphorylates and inhibits glycogen synthase, thereby preventing glycogen synthesis. This coordinated action ensures that the liver releases glucose into the bloodstream during periods of low blood glucose.

Question 4
Core Principle: Constitutively active PP1 would lead to persistent dephosphorylation of glycogen enzymes, promoting synthesis and inhibiting breakdown, even when glucose is scarce.
Target Answer: If protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) were constitutively active in liver cells, it would continuously dephosphorylate glycogen synthase, keeping it in its active state, and continuously dephosphorylate glycogen phosphorylase, keeping it in its inactive state. During a fasting state, when glucagon would normally be high and PKA active, this constitutively active PP1 would counteract the PKA-mediated phosphorylation. Consequently, liver glycogen breakdown would be severely impaired, and glycogen synthesis might even be inappropriately active. This would lead to a failure to release glucose from the liver into the blood, resulting in severe hypoglycemia, as the liver's primary role in maintaining systemic blood glucose homeostasis would be compromised.

Question 5
Core Principle: Liver and muscle tissues have distinct regulatory mechanisms for glycogen metabolism, reflecting their different roles in systemic glucose homeostasis versus local energy supply.
Target Answer: The liver's primary role is to maintain systemic blood glucose homeostasis, releasing glucose into the blood when levels are low. Therefore, liver glycogen phosphorylase is allosterically inhibited by high glucose, promoting storage when glucose is abundant. It also responds strongly to glucagon, which signals low blood glucose. In contrast, muscle glycogen is primarily for the muscle's own local energy needs, especially during activity. Muscle glycogen phosphorylase is allosterically activated by AMP (signaling low energy) and inhibited by ATP and glucose-6-phosphate (signaling high energy). It also responds to epinephrine during "fight-or-flight." This tissue specificity ensures that the liver prioritizes blood glucose levels, while muscle prioritizes its immediate energy demands, preventing dangerous fluctuations in blood sugar (hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia).
