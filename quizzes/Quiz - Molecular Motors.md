Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: Myosin's power stroke is driven by ATP hydrolysis and subsequent Pi release, causing a conformational change that generates force.
Target Answer: Myosin II's head domain binds to actin and ATP. ATP hydrolysis occurs, but Pi remains bound, cocking the head. The release of inorganic phosphate (Pi) from the head domain is the critical event that triggers the power stroke. This release causes a significant conformational change in the neck/linker region, acting as a lever arm, which pulls the actin filament relative to the myosin, generating mechanical force for muscle contraction within the sarcomere. ADP is then released, allowing a new ATP to bind and detach myosin from actin.

Question 2
Core Principle: Eukaryotic motors use ATP hydrolysis, while the bacterial flagellar motor uses an ion gradient for energy transduction.
Target Answer: Eukaryotic molecular motors, such as myosins, kinesins, and dyneins, convert chemical energy into mechanical work primarily through the hydrolysis of ATP. The energy released from breaking the terminal phosphate bond of ATP drives conformational changes in the motor protein, leading to movement along their respective cytoskeletal filaments. In contrast, the bacterial flagellar motor, a prokaryotic motor, does not utilize ATP hydrolysis. Instead, it harnesses the electrochemical potential energy stored in a proton or sodium ion gradient across the bacterial membrane to power the rotation of the flagellum, enabling chemotaxis.

Question 3
Core Principle: Kinesins and dyneins provide directional and cargo-specific transport along microtubules, ensuring proper cellular organization.
Target Answer: Kinesins and dyneins are both microtubule-based motors but exhibit distinct directionality and cargo specificity, which is crucial for organized intracellular transport. Kinesins are primarily responsible for anterograde transport, moving cargo towards the plus end of microtubules (e.g., away from the cell body). Dyneins, conversely, are responsible for retrograde transport, moving cargo towards the minus end of microtubules (e.g., towards the cell body). This opposing directionality, coupled with their specific tail domains for cargo binding, allows the cell to precisely deliver vesicles, organelles, and other cellular components to their correct destinations, maintaining cellular architecture and function. For example, cytoplasmic dyneins transport vesicles towards the cell body, while axonemal dyneins power the beating of cilia and flagella.

Question 4
Core Principle: Failure to release Pi prevents the power stroke, locking myosin in a pre-power stroke state.
Target Answer: If a mutant myosin II motor protein is unable to release inorganic phosphate (Pi) after ATP hydrolysis, the power stroke would be severely impaired or completely prevented. The release of Pi is the critical trigger for the conformational change in the neck/linker region that constitutes the power stroke, pulling the actin filament. Without Pi release, the myosin head would remain in a high-energy, "cocked" state, bound to ADP and Pi, but unable to execute the force-generating movement. This would effectively lock the myosin onto the actin filament in a pre-power stroke conformation, preventing the cyclical detachment, re-attachment, and pulling action necessary for muscle contraction within the sarcomere.

Question 5
Core Principle: Cytoplasmic and axonemal dyneins perform distinct, yet essential, cellular functions by transporting cargo or powering motility.
Target Answer: The functional divergence of cytoplasmic and axonemal dyneins highlights their specialized roles in eukaryotic cells. Cytoplasmic dyneins are primarily involved in intracellular transport, moving vesicles and organelles in a retrograde (minus-end directed) fashion along microtubules, often towards the cell body. This is crucial for maintaining cellular organization and delivering components to specific locations. Axonemal dyneins, on the other hand, are specialized components of cilia and flagella (structures with an axoneme). Their coordinated action powers the rhythmic beating of these appendages, which is essential for cell motility (e.g., sperm movement) or fluid movement across cell surfaces (e.g., respiratory tract clearance). Thus, while both are dyneins, their distinct localization and specific mechanical outputs enable a wide range of essential cellular processes.
