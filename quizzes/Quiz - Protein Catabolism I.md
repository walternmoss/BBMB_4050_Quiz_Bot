Topic: Protein Catabolism I
Quiz Questions

Section 1: Conceptual Practice Questions

Question 1:
How do endopeptidases and exopeptidases, acting in concert during dietary protein digestion, ensure the efficient breakdown of complex proteins into absorbable units? Specify the distinct roles of enzymes like pepsin versus carboxypeptidases.

Question 2:
Protein turnover involves continuous synthesis and degradation. From a cellular resource perspective, explain why the continuous degradation of proteins, even though it consumes energy, is essential for maintaining cellular function and adaptation.

Question 3:
The Ubiquitin-Proteasome System is described as a "major pathway for regulated protein degradation." Mechanistically explain how the specificity and regulation of protein degradation are achieved within this system, highlighting the roles of specific components.

Question 4:
Consider a cell experiencing severe nutrient deprivation. If a genetic mutation impaired the function of the 19S regulatory cap of the proteasome, how would this specifically impact the cell's ability to respond to starvation conditions, and what would be the broader cellular consequences?

Question 5:
Beyond providing amino acids, explain how the coordinated action of the Ubiquitin-Proteasome System and lysosomal degradation pathways (macroautophagy, microautophagy, chaperone-mediated autophagy) collectively contribute to cellular health and adaptation, particularly in preventing cellular damage.

Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: Endopeptidases initiate protein breakdown internally, while exopeptidases complete the process by cleaving from the ends, yielding absorbable units.
Target Answer: Dietary protein digestion begins with endopeptidases like pepsin in the stomach, which hydrolyze internal peptide bonds, breaking large proteins into smaller polypeptides. In the small intestine, other endopeptidases such as trypsin, chymotrypsin, and elastase continue this internal cleavage. Subsequently, exopeptidases, including carboxypeptidases (cleaving from the C-terminus) and aminopeptidases (cleaving from the N-terminus), act on these smaller polypeptides and peptides. This sequential and complementary action ensures that complex proteins are efficiently reduced to free amino acids, dipeptides, and tripeptides, which are the absorbable units.

Question 2
Core Principle: Protein degradation is crucial for cellular function by removing damaged proteins, regulating protein levels, and supplying amino acids for new synthesis and energy during nutrient stress.
Target Answer: Continuous protein degradation is essential because it serves multiple vital functions. Firstly, it removes damaged, misfolded, or dysfunctional proteins, preventing their accumulation which could harm the cell. Secondly, it allows for the rapid regulation of protein levels, enabling cells to quickly adapt to changing environmental conditions by adjusting the abundance of specific proteins. Thirdly, protein breakdown provides a continuous supply of amino acids, which are the necessary building blocks for new protein synthesis. Finally, during periods of nutrient deprivation or starvation, protein degradation becomes a critical source of amino acids for essential metabolic processes, providing a nutrient supply to sustain the cell.

Question 3
Core Principle: Specificity in the Ubiquitin-Proteasome System is primarily conferred by E3 ligases, which target specific proteins for ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation.
Target Answer: Specificity and regulation in the Ubiquitin-Proteasome System are achieved through a multi-step process. First, ubiquitin, a small protein tag, is attached to target proteins. This ubiquitination process involves a cascade of enzymes: E1 (ubiquitin-activating enzyme), E2 (ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme), and crucially, E3 (ubiquitin ligase). The E3 ligases are responsible for recognizing specific target proteins, thereby providing the substrate specificity for degradation. Once a protein is polyubiquitinated, it is recognized by the 19S regulatory cap of the proteasome. The cap then unfolds the ubiquitinated protein and threads it into the 20S proteolytic core, where it is degraded into small peptides. This precise targeting by E3 ligases ensures that only specific, designated proteins are marked for destruction, allowing for tight cellular control over protein abundance.

Question 4
Core Principle: Impaired 19S cap function would prevent the proteasome from degrading ubiquitinated proteins, hindering amino acid supply during starvation and leading to accumulation of dysfunctional proteins.
Target Answer: During severe nutrient deprivation, cells rely on protein breakdown to provide amino acids for essential metabolic processes. The 19S regulatory cap of the proteasome is responsible for recognizing ubiquitinated proteins, unfolding them, and feeding them into the proteolytic core for degradation. If the 19S cap's function is impaired, ubiquitinated proteins would accumulate and not be degraded by the proteasome. This would severely compromise the cell's ability to generate free amino acids from its existing protein pool, exacerbating the nutrient stress. Furthermore, the accumulation of misfolded or dysfunctional proteins that would normally be cleared by the proteasome would lead to cellular toxicity, impaired cellular function, and potentially cell death, as the cell loses its ability to maintain protein homeostasis and adapt to the harsh environment.

Question 5
Core Principle: The Ubiquitin-Proteasome System and lysosomal pathways collaboratively remove damaged or unnecessary proteins and organelles, preventing cellular harm and enabling adaptation.
Target Answer: The Ubiquitin-Proteasome System (UPS) and lysosomal degradation pathways work in concert to maintain cellular health and adaptation by preventing cellular damage. The UPS primarily targets individual, often short-lived or misfolded, proteins for degradation, preventing their accumulation and associated toxicity. Lysosomal degradation, through pathways like macroautophagy, microautophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy, handles a broader range of substrates. Macroautophagy engulfs larger cellular components, including damaged organelles and bulk cytoplasm, within autophagosomes that fuse with lysosomes. Microautophagy involves direct uptake of cytosolic proteins by lysosomes, while chaperone-mediated autophagy specifically transports tagged proteins across the lysosomal membrane. Together, these systems ensure the efficient removal of damaged or dysfunctional proteins and organelles, preventing cellular stress, maintaining protein homeostasis, and allowing the cell to adapt to changing conditions by recycling components and clearing harmful aggregates.
