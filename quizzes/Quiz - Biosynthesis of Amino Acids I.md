Topic: Biosynthesis of Amino Acids I
Quiz Questions
Section 1: Conceptual Practice Questions
Question 1:
A mutant legume plant produces non-functional leghemoglobin, allowing atmospheric oxygen concentrations to enter the infected root nodule tissue containing symbiotic Rhizobium. Explain the immediate biochemical consequence this oxygen exposure has on the nitrogenase enzyme complex and why it irreversibly halts ammonia synthesis.
Question 2:
In the biosynthesis of serine from the glycolytic intermediate 3-phosphoglycerate, the pathway first oxidizes the substrate to 3-phosphohydroxypyruvate before transferring an amino group from glutamate. Explain the chemical necessity of converting the C2 hydroxyl group into a ketone before transamination can occur.
Question 3:
Even though reducing N2 to NH3 is exergonic under physiological conditions, nitrogenase consumes 16 ATP per N2. Explain why this ATP is needed (focusing on the activation energy barrier) and describe how ATP hydrolysis helps transfer electrons through the enzyme.
Question 4:
During rapid cell growth, significant quantities of α-ketoglutarate and oxaloacetate are continuously withdrawn from the citric acid cycle to supply the carbon skeletons for glutamate- and aspartate-family amino acids. Explain how this sustained “cataplerotic depletion” impairs mitochondrial ATP generation if anaplerotic pathways fail to replenish these cycle intermediates.
Question 5:
Explain why glyphosate (Roundup) is an effective broad-spectrum herbicide and antimicrobial agent, yet exhibits low acute toxicity in humans and other animals. In your response, identify the specific pathway involved and the critical metabolic branch-point compound produced.
Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations
Question 1
Core Principle: Metallocluster oxygen sensitivity and irreversible oxidative inactivation of nitrogenase.
Target Answer: Atmospheric oxygen rapidly oxidizes and destroys the oxygen-sensitive iron-sulfur ([4Fe-4S]) clusters of the Fe protein (dinitrogenase reductase) and damages the catalytic FeMo cofactor of the MoFe protein. This oxidative breakdown dismantles the low-potential electron transport pathway between the two enzyme components. Without functional metalloclusters to deliver electrons sequentially to the active site, the complex cannot reduce dinitrogen to ammonia, rendering the system completely inactive.
Question 2
Core Principle: Carbonyl electrophilicity and Schiff base formation in pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)-dependent transamination.
Target Answer: The C2 hydroxyl group of 3-phosphoglycerate lacks sufficient electrophilicity to undergo nucleophilic attack by an incoming amine donor. Oxidizing this secondary alcohol to a ketone creates an electron-deficient carbonyl carbon that readily undergoes nucleophilic attack by the amino group of an enzyme-bound PLP adduct or glutamate. This carbonyl intermediate is chemically indispensable for forming the covalent aldimine (Schiff base) intermediate required to delocalize electrons and complete the transamination reaction to phosphoserine.
Question 3
Core Principle: Overcoming kinetic activation barriers through ATP-induced conformational gating of reduction potentials.
Target Answer: The dinitrogen molecule possesses an exceptionally strong triple bond (N≡N, bond dissociation energy of 945 kJ/mol) that creates a massive kinetic activation energy barrier that favorable overall thermodynamics cannot overcome at physiological temperatures. ATP hydrolysis is not used to pull an unfavorable equilibrium, but rather triggers critical conformational shifts when the Fe protein binds the MoFe protein. This nucleotide-driven conformational gating significantly lowers the redox potential of the Fe protein's cluster, enabling the injection of high-energy electrons into the active site to cleave the triple bond.
Question 4
Core Principle: Catalytic carrier depletion via cataplerosis and the absolute requirement for anaplerosis to sustain oxidative flux.
Target Answer: Citric acid cycle intermediates act catalytically rather than as net stoichiometric fuel, meaning oxaloacetate must be available in each cycle to condense with incoming acetyl-CoA. Cataplerotic siphoning of α-ketoglutarate and oxaloacetate for amino acid synthesis shrinks the total pool of these four- and five-carbon carriers, leaving insufficient oxaloacetate to accept acetyl-CoA. Consequently, flux through the entire cycle stalls, shutting down the generation of NADH and FADH2 and depriving the electron transport chain of the reducing equivalents required for oxidative phosphorylation.
Question 5
Core Principle: Target absence through evolutionary pathway divergence (shikimate pathway inhibition).
Target Answer: Glyphosate targets 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate (EPSP) synthase in the shikimate pathway, which produces chorismate. Chorismate is the essential precursor for the biosynthesis of the aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan). Because animals lack the shikimate/chorismate pathway entirely and must acquire aromatic amino acids through their diet, inhibiting EPSP synthase kills plants and microbes without directly disrupting animal metabolism.
