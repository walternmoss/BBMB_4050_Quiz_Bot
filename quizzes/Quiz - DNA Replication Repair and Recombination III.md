Topic: DNA Replication, Repair, and Recombination III
Quiz Questions

Section 1: Conceptual Practice Questions

Question 1:
The Base Excision Repair (BER) pathway is initiated by DNA glycosylases. Describe the specific molecular event catalyzed by a DNA glycosylase when it encounters a deaminated cytosine (uracil) in the DNA, and explain the immediate consequence of this action for the DNA strand.

Question 2:
UV radiation induces the formation of bulky thymine dimers. Explain the structural impact of a thymine dimer on the DNA double helix and describe the general action of excinucleases in the Nucleotide Excision Repair (NER) pathway to initiate the removal of such a lesion.

Question 3:
DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) are critical lesions repaired by either Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ) or Homologous Recombination Repair (HRR). Based on the provided information, explain the fundamental difference in the *accuracy* of these two pathways and infer a cellular context where one might be favored over the other.

Question 4:
A research team develops a mutant cell line where the MutS protein is completely non-functional. Predict the primary consequence for genomic integrity in this cell line, specifically identifying the type of DNA damage that would accumulate and explaining the molecular role of MutS in preventing this accumulation.

Question 5:
Non-Allelic Homologous Recombination (NAHR) is described as a process that can lead to gene deletions or duplications, resulting in genetic disorders. Based on the principles of homologous recombination and the "non-allelic" nature, explain the molecular mechanism by which NAHR causes these specific large-scale genomic alterations and why such changes are detrimental to cellular function.

Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: DNA glycosylases remove a single damaged base by cleaving the N-glycosidic bond, creating an abasic site.
Target Answer: When a DNA glycosylase encounters a deaminated cytosine (uracil), it specifically recognizes and cleaves the N-glycosidic bond linking the uracil base to the deoxyribose sugar in the DNA backbone. This action removes only the damaged base, leaving the sugar-phosphate backbone intact but creating an abasic (AP) site, which is a site lacking a purine or pyrimidine base. This AP site then serves as an intermediate for further repair steps in the BER pathway.

Question 2
Core Principle: Thymine dimers create a bulky lesion that distorts the DNA helix, and excinucleases remove a segment of the DNA strand containing this lesion.
Target Answer: A thymine dimer is a covalent linkage between two adjacent thymine bases on the same DNA strand, induced by UV radiation. This bulky lesion causes a significant distortion in the DNA double helix, disrupting the normal base pairing and the smooth helical structure. Excinucleases, key enzymes in the Nucleotide Excision Repair (NER) pathway, recognize this distortion and make two phosphodiester bond cuts: one on either side of the bulky lesion. This excises a short oligonucleotide segment containing the thymine dimer, creating a gap that can then be filled by DNA polymerase and ligase.

Question 3
Core Principle: NHEJ is an error-prone pathway that directly ligates broken ends, while HRR is an accurate pathway that uses a homologous template.
Target Answer: The fundamental difference lies in their accuracy: Non-Homologous End Joining (NHEJ) is described as error-prone because it directly ligates the broken DNA ends without requiring a homologous template, often leading to small deletions or insertions at the repair site. In contrast, Homologous Recombination Repair (HRR) is an accurate repair mechanism because it utilizes a homologous chromosome or sister chromatid as a template to precisely restore the original DNA sequence. Therefore, NHEJ might be preferentially utilized when a homologous template is unavailable (e.g., in G1 phase of the cell cycle), prioritizing rapid repair over perfect accuracy to prevent catastrophic chromosome loss. HRR would be favored when a sister chromatid is present (e.g., in S or G2 phase), allowing for high-fidelity repair.

Question 4
Core Principle: A non-functional MutS protein would lead to the accumulation of replication errors due to impaired Mismatch Repair (MMR).
Target Answer: MutS is a crucial component of the Mismatch Repair (MMR) pathway, which is responsible for fixing replication errors, such as incorrectly incorporated bases that escape the proofreading activity of DNA polymerase. If the MutS protein is completely non-functional, the cell would be unable to recognize and initiate the repair of these replication errors. Consequently, the primary consequence for genomic integrity would be the accumulation of unrepaired base mismatches and small insertion/deletion loops that arise during DNA replication, leading to a significantly increased mutation rate in the cell line.

Question 5
Core Principle: NAHR involves recombination between misaligned, non-allelic homologous sequences, leading to unequal exchange and resulting in gene deletions or duplications.
Target Answer: Non-Allelic Homologous Recombination (NAHR) occurs when recombination takes place between homologous DNA sequences that are not perfectly aligned or are located at different positions on homologous chromosomes (i.e., non-allelic). This misalignment during recombination leads to an unequal exchange of genetic material. Specifically, one recombinant chromosome will end up with a duplication of the region between the misaligned homologous sequences, while the other recombinant chromosome will suffer a deletion of that same region. Such large-scale genomic alterations, involving the gain or loss of entire genes or gene segments, are detrimental to cellular function because they disrupt gene dosage, potentially leading to overexpression or underexpression of critical proteins, which can manifest as severe genetic disorders.
