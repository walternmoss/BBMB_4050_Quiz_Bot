Section 2: Answer Key & Mechanistic Explanations

Question 1
Core Principle: Amino groups are removed from amino acids via transamination or oxidative deamination, each employing specific enzymes and cofactors.
Target Answer: Amino groups are removed from amino acids through two primary mechanisms. Transamination involves aminotransferases, which require PLP (pyridoxal phosphate) as a cofactor. In this process, the amino group from an amino acid is transferred to a keto acid, resulting in a new keto acid and a new amino acid. The second mechanism is oxidative deamination, primarily catalyzed by glutamate dehydrogenase. This enzyme directly removes the amino group from glutamate as free ammonia.

Question 2
Core Principle: The urea cycle detoxifies highly toxic ammonia by converting it into less toxic urea for excretion.
Target Answer: The fundamental biological problem necessitating the urea cycle is the high toxicity of ammonia, which is generated during amino acid deamination. Ammonia is particularly harmful to the central nervous system. The urea cycle addresses this problem by converting toxic ammonia into urea, a much less toxic compound that can be safely transported in the blood and excreted by the kidneys. This liver-based pathway combines ammonia with bicarbonate and aspartate through a series of reactions involving key intermediates such as ornithine, citrulline, argininosuccinate, and arginine, ultimately forming urea.

Question 3
Core Principle: Amino acid carbon skeletons are classified based on their metabolic fate, either contributing to glucose synthesis (glucogenic) or ketone body synthesis (ketogenic).
Target Answer: The classification of amino acid carbon skeletons as glucogenic or ketogenic dictates their ultimate metabolic fate. Glucogenic amino acids have carbon skeletons that can be converted into intermediates that are precursors for glucose synthesis, primarily through gluconeogenesis. Examples include alanine and glutamate, whose carbon skeletons can enter the citric acid cycle and eventually be used to produce glucose. Ketogenic amino acids, such as leucine and lysine, have carbon skeletons that are used to synthesize ketone bodies or acetyl-CoA. These carbon skeletons cannot be directly converted to glucose but can be utilized for energy production via the citric acid cycle or for ketogenesis.

Question 4
Core Principle: Elevated blood ammonia (hyperammonemia) can result from defects in the urea cycle or impaired liver function.
Target Answer: Elevated blood ammonia, or hyperammonemia, can arise from two distinct metabolic defects. First, defects in the urea cycle itself can impair the body's ability to convert toxic ammonia into urea for excretion. Since the urea cycle is the primary pathway for ammonia detoxification, a defect in any of its enzymes or transport systems would lead to the accumulation of ammonia in the bloodstream. Second, significant liver dysfunction can also cause hyperammonemia because the urea cycle is a liver-based pathway. If the liver's overall metabolic capacity is compromised due to disease or damage, its ability to carry out the urea cycle effectively will be diminished, leading to a buildup of ammonia.

Question 5
Core Principle: Defects in branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) metabolism lead to the accumulation of toxic intermediates, as exemplified by Maple Syrup Urine Disease (MSUD).
Target Answer: Branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs), which include leucine, isoleucine, and valine, are primarily metabolized in the muscle. The clinical significance of defects in BCAA metabolism is profound, as these defects lead to the accumulation of toxic BCAA metabolites in the body. A specific disease example is Maple Syrup Urine Disease (MSUD), which is caused by a defect in the catabolism of BCAAs. In MSUD, the impaired breakdown of these amino acids results in their buildup in the blood and urine, leading to severe neurological damage, developmental delays, and other serious health problems if not managed through strict dietary intervention.
